What is Fontis?

Fontis is Latin for "of the source".

A paper is supposed to be a record of how we reached some state of knowledge. It follows from the observations and papers before it, and with luck it becomes a building block for later work. Ideally, following its citations as far as they go would end in hard evidence: something we can check with our senses, or logically with a proof. Often it doesn't. Trails stop at assumptions, or at citations that lead nowhere.

Many in academia have called for a cleanup of citation and link rot, and those calls have mostly gone unheeded. In this snapshot of Attention Is All You Need, about a quarter of the papers four levels down are dead ends: a dead ID, or no references on record. Some of those are gaps in the database rather than in the paper, and telling them apart is part of the work.

Now LLMs are generating papers at a rapid clip, and academics acknowledge that verification is falling behind. LLMs also famously hallucinate citations. One hypothesis: they learned it partly from us, where a citation can point at nothing and nobody checks. We were the model.

So how would we even find the rot? Think of a citation as a dependency. In software, a project is only as reliable as its dependency tree, and securing that supply chain is core engineering work. A paper's citations, and their citations, all the way down, are its dependencies. For knowledge, we have almost no tooling to audit that chain.

This demo shows one way to start building it. Lay out the citation trail all the way down, and a claim can be checked by checking the history it's built on.

Everything shown is measured from a dated OpenAlex snapshot. Nothing here says whether a paper is right on its own. It shows what a paper rests on, and where the trail stops.

Git records every commit's parents. Papers record theirs in the bibliography. Fontis draws those links as a commit graph, so a lineage reads like git log --graph.

  • Commit: a paper.
  • Parents: its references. Only the most cited are drawn; the controls change how many, and +n on a row shows all of that paper's.
  • Merge commit: a paper with several parents, which is nearly all of them.
  • Shared ancestor: a paper where lines join, because several papers in view cite it.
  • Spine: the straight line down the left, following each paper's most cited parent from the seed.
  • Turning the graph: drag across it, or use the slider. Lanes sit on a loop, and the table lists the papers on the lanes in view. Fixed rows gives every paper its own row instead.
  • Tail: a link that leaves the view. Hover it to see where it goes, or click it to go there. Clicking a paper brings its line back to the seed into view.
  • Full tree: the entire graph as a spinning cone, with the seed at the tip, older papers further down, and one block of lanes per reference. The table is the strip facing you, like the part of a tire touching the ground.
  • Fading line: the paper has references this view doesn't reach. The trail carries on.
  • Suspect link: a paper dated more than two years after one citing it, which usually means a bad match or a wrong date.
  • Dead ID: a reference whose OpenAlex record returns 404. The work may exist elsewhere; the link is what's gone.
